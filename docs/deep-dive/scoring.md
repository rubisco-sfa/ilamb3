# Scoring

A {term}`score` is a monotonic function $s$
that maps an error $\varepsilon$ to a value from 0 to 1, where 1 is perfect.
The error measures the difference between the comparison and the reference
and is 0 when they match.
The score is a nonincreasing function of the error:
a larger error never receives a higher score.
Most `ilamb3` scores
use an exponential score function

$$s(\varepsilon) = e^{-\alpha\varepsilon},$$

where $\alpha > 0$ sets how quickly the score decreases as the error increases.

Because $s$ is nonincreasing,
scoring never reverses the ranking of models that each have a single error.
In practice, however, a score often summarizes many errors:
a regional score summarizes the errors in every cell of a region,
and some scores also summarize errors over years or river basins.
Earlier versions of ILAMB scored each error and averaged the scores
([Collier et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1029/2018MS001354), Eq. 15).
Because $s$ is nonlinear,
the ranking of models by that average
depends on the form of $s$ and on $\alpha$,
not only on the errors,
so changing the score function could reorder models
whose errors had not changed.
`ilamb3` instead aggregates the errors and scores the result once,
which makes the ranking of models invariant
to the choice of score function and its parameters.

:::{important} Change from earlier versions
Scores that earlier versions formed by averaging scores
have different values in this version,
so they should not be compared across versions.
The Seasonal Cycle Score, $1 - \lvert\text{shift}\rvert / 6$,
and the Dispersion Score, one minus the Hellinger distance,
are unaffected,
because they are affine in their errors:
`ilamb3` scores the regional mean error,
and both orders give the same value.
The Spatial Distribution, Time Series, Relationship, and Area scores
are also unaffected,
because they are computed from statistics of the whole region
and average no scores.
:::

## Order of operations

Let $\varepsilon_i$ be the error in cell $i$ of a region,
and let $\langle \cdot \rangle$ denote the area-weighted mean over the region.
A regional score can be formed in two orders:

1. **Score, then average:** $\langle s(\varepsilon_i) \rangle$.
2. **Aggregate, then score:** $s(E)$,
   where $E$ is a single error aggregated over the region.

The mean score equals the score of the mean error,
$s(\langle \varepsilon_i \rangle)$,
for every map of errors if and only if $s$ is affine,
$s(\varepsilon) = a + b\varepsilon$.
The exponential score is not affine,
so the order matters.

Score-then-average is itself a way of aggregating the errors.
If $s$ is strictly decreasing, as the exponential score is, it has an inverse,
and the mean score is the score of a single number,

$$\langle s(\varepsilon_i) \rangle = s(M_s), \qquad M_s = s^{-1}\big(\langle s(\varepsilon_i) \rangle\big),$$

where $M_s$ is the quasi-arithmetic mean of the errors generated by $s$
(Hardy, Littlewood, and Pólya, *Inequalities*, 2nd ed., 1952, Ch. III).
For the exponential score, $M_s$ is the exponential mean

$$M_\alpha = -\frac{1}{\alpha} \ln \langle e^{-\alpha\varepsilon_i} \rangle.$$

$M_\alpha$ approaches the arithmetic mean of the errors as $\alpha \to 0$,
is nonincreasing in $\alpha$,
and approaches the smallest error in the region as $\alpha \to \infty$.
Under score-then-average,
the parameter of the score function
therefore also sets how the errors are averaged:
the larger $\alpha$ is, the more a model's smallest errors determine its score,
and two models can exchange ranks as $\alpha$ changes.

Aggregate-then-score fixes $E$ before the score function is applied.
Every strictly decreasing score function, with any parameters,
then ranks models in order of increasing $E$,
so the ranking of models is invariant to the choice of score function.
A score function that is only nonincreasing, such as one clipped at 0,
can tie models but cannot reverse their order.
`ilamb3` therefore aggregates first and scores once.

## What is aggregated

For each cell $i$ with area weight $w_i$,
an analysis computes an error $e_i$ and a normalizer $n_i$ in the same units.
`ilamb3` sums their squares over the region, weighted by $w_i$,
and scores the square root of the ratio of the two sums:

$$E = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_i w_i e_i^2}{\sum_i w_i n_i^2}}, \qquad \text{score} = s(E).$$

Cells where either square is missing are left out of both sums.
With mass weighting,
$w_i$ is also multiplied by the temporal mean of the reference.

| Analysis | Error $e_i$ | Normalizer $n_i$ |
|---|---|---|
| Bias | $\lvert \text{bias} \rvert$, less the reference uncertainty | reference standard deviation in time (the reference mean if it has no time dimension), or a regional quantile |
| RMSE | centralized RMSE, less the reference uncertainty | centralized RMS of the reference |
| Hydro | $\lvert \text{difference} \rvert$ of an annual or seasonal mean | reference standard deviation in time, of annual means or of the values within the season |

$E$ is therefore the ratio of the root-mean-square error
to the root-mean-square normalizer.
For the RMSE score,
whose value in each cell is already the root of a mean over time,
the root-mean-square error in $E$ is taken over all times and cells,
which is the definition used by the WMO and ECMWF
([Rasp et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1029/2023MS004019)).
Squaring gives large errors more weight
than averaging the scores did.

Pooling the normalizer,
rather than dividing each cell's error by its own normalizer before averaging,
weights each squared relative error $(e_i/n_i)^2$ by $w_i n_i^2$,
so a cell with near-zero variability, such as a desert,
cannot dominate the regional error.
The overall mean squared skill score of the
[WMO standard for verifying long-range forecasts](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/binaries/content/assets/metofficegovuk/pdf/research/climate-science/climate-observations-projections-and-impacts/svslrf.pdf)
(WMO-No. 485, Attachment II.8, Sec. 3.3.1)
pools in the same way:
for the RMSE score without the uncertainty discount,
$E^2$ is one minus that skill score computed on anomalies.

## Local scores

Regional scores are computed from aggregated errors,
but `ilamb3` also writes score maps,
in which the error in each cell is scored separately.
These local scores show where a model's errors lie,
but averaging a score map over a region
would compute a regional score in the score-then-average order.
To prevent this,
`ilamb3` marks every score map with the attribute `local_score = 1`,
which is saved in the output netCDF files.
Every function in `ilamb3.dataset` that reduces data over space, time, or depth,
as well as `ilamb3.analysis.base.scalarify` and `ilamb3.analysis.scoring.aggregate`,
raises `ilamb3.exceptions.ScoreAggregationError` when given a marked array,
with a message that says how to compute the regional score instead.
Because `ilamb3` keeps attributes through xarray operations,
an array derived from a score map, such as `1 - score`, is also refused.
Reductions called directly on xarray objects,
such as `score.mean()`, are not checked.

The Bias, RMSE, and Hydro analyses save each score map
with the statistics it was computed from,
in a variable with the suffix `_statistics`, such as `biasscore_statistics`.
A regional score can then be recomputed exactly from an analysis output file:

```python
import xarray as xr
from ilamb3.analysis import scoring

ds = xr.load_dataset("MODEL.nc")  # the comparison output of an analysis
stats = scoring.get_statistics(ds, "biasscore")
value = float(scoring.score(scoring.aggregate(stats, region=None)))
```

With mass weighting, pass the same weight to `aggregate`.

An analysis that adds a score should follow the same pattern:

```python
from ilamb3.analysis import scoring

# com is the dataset on whose grid the error is defined, such as a nested grid;
# its cell bounds give each cell its area when the statistics are aggregated
error = (np.abs(com[varname] - ref[varname]) - uncertainty).clip(0)
stats = scoring.statistics(error, ref_std, grid=com)

# A local score map, saved with its statistics
out_com = scoring.add_score(out_com, "myscore", stats)

# A regional score: aggregate the statistics, then score them once
for region in regions:
    value = float(scoring.score(scoring.aggregate(stats, region)))
```
